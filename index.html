📄 Read — Then Answer the Questions
Peer Revision Scenario A student named Marcus is writing an article for the school newspaper. His research question is: Should our school start a recycling program? He is writing to persuade other students and the school principal. Marcus has finished his first draft and asked you to be his peer reviewer. Read his two sources and his draft, then answer the questions about how to revise it.
Our School Needs Recycling
First draft by Marcus · sentences are numbered for peer review
Paragraph 1
1Recycling is a thing that a lot of people talk about these days. 2There are many opinions about recycling in schools. 3Our school should probably think about starting a recycling program.
Paragraph 2
4One reason is that Americans throw away a huge amount of trash every single day. 5Most of that trash goes to landfills. 6It sits there for a very long time. 7They say landfills make gases that are bad for the air. 8That is a big problem.

Source 1: "What Happens to What We Throw Away"
Informational Text

Every year, people in the United States generate about 292 million tons of municipal solid waste — roughly 4.9 pounds per person every day. That includes paper, food scraps, plastic containers, yard trimmings, metal, and glass.

  • Only about 32% of that waste is recycled or composted. Close to half of it is buried in landfills.
  • As buried waste breaks down, it releases methane, a greenhouse gas that traps heat in the atmosphere. Landfills are the third-largest source of human-related methane emissions in the United States.
  • Recycling one ton of paper saves about 17 trees and roughly 7,000 gallons of water.
  • Aluminum can be recycled again and again without losing quality. Recycling a single aluminum can saves enough energy to run a television for about three hours.

Reducing what we send to landfills conserves natural resources and keeps greenhouse gases out of the air.

Source 2: "Schools That Made Recycling Work"
Informational Text
  • Ridgeview Middle School in Ohio cut the waste it sent to landfills by 41% in a single year after students designed and ran their own recycling program.
  • The district spent about $1,200 on collection bins and saved about $3,400 in trash-hauling fees during the first year.
  • Researchers who studied 60 schools found that schools where students ran the program recycled about twice as much as schools where only custodians handled recycling.
  • Some schools report that collecting and sorting takes 10 to 15 minutes of class time per week.

Not everyone is convinced. Dr. Elena Ruiz, a waste-management researcher, points out that about 25% of what people put in recycling bins is contaminated with food or the wrong kinds of plastic, and badly contaminated loads are sometimes sent to a landfill anyway. She argues that schools should teach students what belongs in each bin before the bins arrive.

Sources are instructional texts written for this practice task. Figures in Source 1 reflect published national waste data; the school in Source 2 is a composite example.
